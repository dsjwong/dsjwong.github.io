The retrain that looked worse, and wasn’t

A live number fell from 0.718 to 0.621 after a refit. Whether to roll back depended on which flights I compared.

My delay model retrains every week. In early November the report card had a row for each model version, and the table said something alarming. The model from 29 October scored an AUC of 0.718 on 1,244 flights. The model refitted on 1 November scored 0.621 on 1,228. A drop of 0.1 after a routine refit looks like a bug.

Same flights, both models

The two rows covered different days, so they were not a like-for-like comparison. I wrote a harness that re-scores any flight as it would have been scored at a given moment: it removes every actual departure after that moment, drops weather reports after it, and runs the same serving path. Then I ran both models on exactly the same flights.

On the same four days, 1 to 4 November, both models scored about 0.63 to 0.65. On 29 October to 1 November both scored about 0.69 to 0.70. The airline-and-hour lookup baseline fell by the same amount between the windows, from 0.671 to 0.621. Daily AUC at around 400 flights has a standard error near 0.03, so a single row of one version in one window is not something to compare with another row.

Over 29 October to 4 November, 2,890 flights, the newer model was slightly better: AUC +0.010 with an interval of +0.003 to +0.017, and better log loss and Brier score with intervals clear of zero. I made no rollback.

A weakness in the gate

The comparison also exposed a problem with the gate that decides whether a new model ships. It evaluated on rows that carried same-day statistics and aircraft-arrival information that a day-ahead prediction does not have, so its AUC level, 0.713, was more flattering than what the live service achieves, between 0.65 and 0.67. A candidate that is worse day-ahead could still pass.

So I added a sixth rule. Both models are now scored day-ahead on the last seven complete days that neither was fitted on, and a ship is blocked only if the confidence interval on the AUC change, or on log loss, lies entirely on the wrong side of zero. I tested it on the two models I had: the November model passes against the October one, and the October model, if proposed as a replacement, is held.
