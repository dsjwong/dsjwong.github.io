Six models, twelve pests, one semester in Sydney

My exchange project: which architecture is best at telling a weevil from a wasp, and where all of them fail.

From August to December 2025 I was on exchange at UNSW in Sydney, on a HKUST Fund Exchange Scholarship. [TBC: Darren: which course the pest project belonged to, who you worked with, and one thing about Sydney that is not on a CV.]

My computer vision project there was a benchmark on the Kaggle crop pests dataset, which has twelve classes: ants, bees, beetles, caterpillars, earthworms, earwigs, grasshoppers, moths, slugs, snails, wasps and weevils. I trained six architectures head to head: three detectors (YOLO11n, Faster R-CNN and EfficientDet) and three classifiers (a Vision Transformer, EfficientNet and ConvNeXt).

The plumbing

Most of the work was not the models. The detectors expect different annotation formats, so I wrote a YOLO-to-COCO conversion. Training used early stopping, and I added explainability visualisations to see what each one was looking at. I also made a deliberately imbalanced split to test how each model behaved when some classes are rare.

What came out

The Vision Transformer reached 93.4% accuracy as a classifier. The YOLOv11 detector reached 0.825 mAP at an IoU of 0.5. The two are not directly comparable, because one is answering “what is in this photo?” and the other “where, and what?”.

The more useful result was the confusion matrix. Moths (0.98), snails (0.95) and weevils (0.95) came out cleanly, and wasps and bees were close behind at 0.89 and 0.85. Caterpillars (0.48) and earthworms (0.47) were the hard classes. Most of their error was confusion with background rather than with each other.

One reading of that is that more model capacity would not help a class the detector cannot separate from the background, and that better data for the hard cases probably would. [TBC: Darren: did you test that, or is it only an inference? Say which, or cut the paragraph.]
